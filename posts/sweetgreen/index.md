# Sweetgreen

## TL;DR

- Sweetgreen (NYSE: SG) is a US fast-casual salad chain of roughly 285 restaurants. Despite having no international presence, the company boasts a ~$1bn market cap. After its first full year of adjusted EBITDA profitability in FY2024, it has gone sharply into reverse, with a FY2025 net loss of about $134m and a same-store sales decline of 12.8% in the first quarter of 2026, its steepest since the 2021 IPO.
- Whilst a successful turnaround would return the company to a maturing home market, an internal expansion could be exciting. The U.K. would be a natural first market, where the premium health-led food-to-go category is growing faster than the wider eating-out sector. The US brands that entered the U.K. organically have stayed small, such as Chipotle reaching only around 19 sites since 2010, while those that entered through a local operating partner have scaled fast: Wingstop UK reached 57 sites and a £150m revenue run-rate under a master franchisee before selling for more than £400m.
- In my view, Sweetgreen's U.K. entry should be a merger that leaves a local founder team running the business as owners, in preference to a greenfield build or a master-franchise of an unknown-in-the-UK brand. I would expect an enterprise value of roughly £60m–£120m (~$80m–$160m), in line with the 2.7x revenue that Wingstop UK fetched, structured predominantly in Sweetgreen stock with founder rollover and an earn-out.

## The Company

### Background

- Sweetgreen was founded in 2006 and 2007 by Jonathan Neman, Nicolas Jammet and Nathaniel Ru, three Georgetown classmates who opened a 560-square-foot salad shop in Washington DC three months after graduating, and it now operates more than 250 restaurants across 24 states and the District of Columbia, listed on the New York Stock Exchange since November 2021.
- The product is a customisable, made-from-scratch bowl built around vegetables and a protein, sold to white-collar workers in dense urban markets at lunch. The menu is narrow and premium by design, kept under 800 calories with no added sugar, which is the source of the chain's cultural standing and also the reason its addressable demand is concentrated in affluent, urban, health-led lunch rather than the broad fast-food market.
- Sweetgreen has noticeably never operated outside the US, nor has it ever franchised, so it has neither foreign operating experience nor any franchising apparatus to lean on in a market where the brand is unknown.

### Financials

- FY2024 was its first full year of adjusted EBITDA profitability, with revenue near $677m, same-store sales up 6%, and a restaurant-level profit margin of 19.6%. I consider this to be the baseline given the rapid reversal ever since.
- In FY2025, full-year revenue was broadly flat at ~$680m, but the net loss deepened to more than $130m, loss from operations was ~$139m, adjusted EBITDA turned negative, the full-year restaurant-level margin compressed to 15.2% from 19.6%, and Q4 showed a same-store sales decline of 11.5% with the quarterly margin falling to 10.4%.
- Q1 2026 was worse on the surface, with revenue of $161.5m, a same-store sales decline of 12.8% and traffic down 11.2%. I have decided to discount the reported net income of $125.8m in that quarter, since it was driven by a one-time gain on the sale of Spyce to Wonder Group, rather than operations. That sale, at $186m including $100m in cash, was completed just after year-end and sat against a cash balance that had fallen to $93.3m; so, liquidity is real but is being held against a business that is currently consuming cash.
- The equity is depressed and volatile, down roughly 80% from its 2024 peak before recovering through 2026 as JPMorgan upgraded the stock to Overweight with a $13 target, citing the reception to the new Wraps line; at around $10 a share that leaves a market capitalisation near $1bn and makes Sweetgreen stock a weak acquisition currency. Management, meanwhile, is fully absorbed by a domestic turnaround it calls the Sweet Growth Transformation Plan, having just refreshed its C-suite.

## Market positioning

- Until now, Sweetgreen's growth model has rested on two engines: new US units and compounding same-store sales. However, opening more US restaurants into falling domestic traffic adds revenue while diluting the store-level economics the company spent years assembling. Even a fully successful turnaround returns Sweetgreen to a maturing domestic market rather than to a new source of growth; I feel the weakness of the US business justifies the rationale for international expansion.
- The fast-food premium healthy eating category is already proven by a cohort of local operators, including atis, The Salad Project, Farmer J and Tossed. This removes the demand risk, because the field tray/build-your-own salad bar plainly works. However, there is an access risk where the major constraint is distribution on two fronts: prime sites in the handful of central London micro-markets where existing players are concentrated, and a brand that a greenfield entrant would have to build from scratch.

## Entry

- I would recommend an inorganic entry especially given how the brands that have previously entered organically have struggled. Chipotle, for example, opened its first London restaurant in 2010 and has reached just around 20 UK locations, with full year revenue of £34.6m in 2024 and a loss before tax of £18m, widening from a £12.5m loss in 2023. The brands that entered through a local operating partner have fared better: Wingstop UK, run since 2018 by the master franchisee Lemon Pepper Holdings, reached 57 sites and an expected £150m revenue run-rate and changed hands for more than £400m, while Popeyes UK grew revenue from just over £58m in 2023 to more than £118m in 2024. From such similar case studies, we can reasonably deduce that perhaps US fast-casual scales when a local team owns the build and stalls when the parent tries to run it from abroad.
- I still reject the franchise route, for two reasons. Sweetgreen has never franchised and operates a wholly company-owned estate, so it would have to construct a franchising capability, a disclosure and support organisation and a packaged set of unit economics before it could even begin, which is itself a multi-year distraction. And even where it works, the franchise route still requires an estate to be built from zero, which is the very thing that takes six or seven years.
- The merger I am envisaging is a majority-stock combination that rolls the target's founders and operating team into Sweetgreen equity and runs the UK as a semi-autonomous business under its existing leadership. This conserves cash and keeps dilution in the order of single-digit to low-double-digit percent against a roughly $1bn market cap.

## Potential targets

- The Salad Project (TSP) is the strongest fit for this merger. Founded in 2021 by Florian de Chezelles and James Dare in Spitalfields, it has built a cult following for a curated, creative, health-focused lunch and grown to over a dozen London sites. It sells fully customisable salads and protein bowls made from scratch, and its positioning, that salad can be a lifestyle, is the closest thing in London to Sweetgreen's own.
- Alternatives such as Farmer J and atis are less suitable. Farmer J's £17.5m round in October 2025 was explicitly raised to fund a New York debut alongside further London openings; this converts Farmer J from a potential target into a direct competitor on Sweetgreen's home turf. atis is the closest of the three to Sweetgreen in format, but the business remains founder-controlled and lightly institutionalised; this makes a clean stock combination harder to negotiate, and means there is no institutional investor on a clock with an incentive to sell.

## Thesis
- Sweetgreen should acquire TSP as its route to U.K. market entry. In my view, it is the most suitable premium salad and bowl operator with a made-from-scratch, design-led, lifestyle identity. TSP secured £9m in January 2026, from investors including Will Shu and Nick Jones, to fund a continental European expansion beginning with a first overseas site in the Le Sentier district of Paris, having previously set a target of 20 sites by mid-2027. The same round brought in the private equity firm Active Partners, with companies like LEON, Caravan, and Chicken Shop within its portfolio. An institutional sponsor that exists to sell, on a clock, at a defined return, makes an M&A process rational and timely, which is a practical reason TSP may be considered a seller by design where the other two are not.
- Sweetgreen brings a category-defining name and listed-company capital that accelerate access and fund multiple sites quicker than a £9m round allows; TSP brings the operating leverage including a proven, de-risked concept at local unit economics, an impending Paris launch, and founder-operators who stay and run it.
- On consideration, I would split it by counterparty. The investors would be presumably satisfied with cash—drawn modestly against the post-Spyce liquidity—possibly alongside a collar that caps the downside on any $SG stock received. The founders could be paid predominantly in Sweetgreen equity with a meaningful earn-out tied to UK and European unit growth.
